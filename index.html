About

I have a PhD in Philosophy from Rutgers University. I have done coursework and research in philosophy at UChicago, Cambridge (UK), UConn, and Notre Dame. I am an analytic philosopher, specializing in language and logic but also highly interested in metaphysics, epistemology, and occasionally even beyond.

I was born in Albania, raised in Greece, and I now live in New York City.

› for non-specialists

I care about what it means to mean. Sometimes I think of myself as a chemist, but for language. Just as a chemist wants to know the laws by which molecules come together, and don't, I want to know the laws by which meanings come together, and don't. Consider this sentence:

Eno was in Bali in September of 2026.

You have almost certainly never encountered that sentence before. Yet you almost certainly understood it immediately. Somehow, you're able to take your knowledge of the meanings of its parts, together with your knowledge of how those parts are put together, and arrive at, indeed construct, a meaning for the whole.

But what exactly are the laws governing that process? For not all meanings can be put together. Consider:

More people have been to Bali than I have.

Someone has ever been to Bali.

Grammatically, these looks fine. But somehow, the meanings of the individual words and phrases fail to stick together into a meaningful sentence; they resist being assembled into a coherent whole.

My work asks what governs successes and failures of this kind, especially when it comes to expressions of possibility and necessity—words such as must and if- and reports of other people's thoughts-like "John believes that ...". I want to know what they contribute to the sentences that contain them, and what principles constrain the ways those meanings combine.

› for specialists

For those in the know: my main research is in formal semantics, with forrays into syntax, pragmatics, and logic. I work especially on intensional expressions—epistemic modals, propositional attitude reports, grammatical mood, conditionals, and tense—and on their interaction with reference and quantification. A central strand of my work concerns binding into intensional contexts. I develop a two-dimensional approach on which natural-language variables can range over two-dimensional semantic objects. I extend this to singular terms, tense, and modality.

In the course of this main research, I have produced four more offshoot lines:

(i) Conditionals: I have developed a referential theory of conditionals, treating conditional antecedents as plural Russellian definite descriptions of possible worlds. Among other things, this gives rise to an unusual and, I think, philosophically interesting logic of conditionals.

(ii) Retraction: Dynamic semantics has a lot to say about epistmeic modals, but surprisingly little about retracting assertions that contain them. I propose a new theory of how dynamic semantics can handle such assertions.

(iii) Linguistic Akrasia: There has always been talk of the normativity of meaning. So, if meaning is normative, there must be linguistic mistakes. And if there can be linguistic mistakes, then there can be such mistakes committed in the knowledge of the fact that they are mistakes. I wonder if this constitutes a distinct case of semantic or pragmatic akrasia.

(iv) Akrasia in AI models: relatedly, I wonder if AI agents are genuinely capable of morally and epsitemically akratic behavior. If they are, as they seem to, my work asks how such akratic behavior can be both tamed and leveraged in the service of alignment.

My work also sometimes veers into neighboring areas of philosophy, especially metaphysics. I also have a longstanding interest in non-classical logic, particularly relevance and connexive logics. Sometimes, I also dabble in Wittgenstein (especially his theory of nonsense).

Poetry

I have another life in language. My first book of poetry, Ποιητικό Αίτιο (Poetic Cause), was published in 2015 and received the Varveris Prize in 2016. My second, Παράξενα Φρούτα που Κρέμονται (Strange Fruit Hanging), appeared in 2023. A third book is in the making.

Analytic Philosophy Salon

I also run The Analytic Salon, a public-facing discussion series in New York built around a simple idea: analytic philosophy can be rigorous without being cloistered. Each session begins with a problem worth getting stuck on, usually introduced by an invited philosopher, and then opens into structured conversation with the room. We have discussed topics ranging from decision theory to metaphysics, with an emphasis on clarity, disagreement, and the pleasure of thinking together.

Are you a student?

I’ve been a philosophy student for most of my life. It was tough much of the time, mainly because I had no guidance and had to figure it out mostly on my own—from whether to study philosophy, to how to do philosophy, to how to craft a successful application for philosophy grad school. I want to give back some of what I learned.

If you are a student—in high school, college, or graduate school—feel free to email me. I can offer general advice, read a personal statement, and give feedback on a writing sample. I have transferred across three PhD programs, so I can also help with that.
